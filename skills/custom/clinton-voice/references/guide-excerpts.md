## Search Methods (Methods.html)

> The search method you employ comes from what information you start with. If you want to find subjects in a predetermined area, a lawnmover pattern is used. If you only have a point of interest or last known position, you would use a bicycle wheel pattern.

> There are a number of methods and tools that search teams use when finding subjects. This is particular in the search for nests and animals on farm land. It is important to match the coordination method with the subject that is being sought.

> **Hover over subject.** When the drone operator finds a possible subject, they hover the drone above it and allows the ground teams to find it by directing them to the exact location. This is the most time consuming method because it means the drone cannot continue searching for other subjects while directing to that subject. It is reliable because if there is a heat signature in long grass and the operator cannot determine what it is, this method gives certainty in a relatively short period of time.

> This method is acceptable when searching for subjects in long grass, if other tools are not available and if the search area is relatively small. Eggs don't produce much heat by themselves, they retain heat from the parent. This is discernable for about 30 minutes after the parent leaves the nest. In the Netherlands, searches start at civil twilight just before sunrise and continue while there is no direct sun on the ground. This means that there is about 90 minutes to cover a search area. This method of coordination is only suitable for areas under 8ha. Taking time away from searching by hovering means more battery drain, more mattery changes and losing search time to hovering over a single subject.

> **Follow with thermal camera.** [...] This method also stretches the possible search area to about 10ha with fewer than 4 possible subjects. Coordinating larger groups of ground teams becomes onerous as communications can get mixed up and increases the load on the operator.

> **Live video in the field.** [...] This is a niche method of searching because it relies on transmission of live video at low latency. It's expensive in terms of equipment [...]. It allows more people to be involved but does not increase the search area that can be covered or reduce the time to get to subjects. This method is used during demonstrations or training.

> **Mark and move on.** This method means that the ground teams and drone searches are completely decoupled from each other. A drone operator marks the location, takes a picture of the subject and moves on immediately. This allows the ground team to evaluate the subject at any time and go to the location at their convenience. This allows the drone to cover the maximum amount of area because there is no delay waiting on any other team member.

> This allows the the search area to be stretched to 15 to 20ha, depending on the number of subjects. This method is also used when there are very few team members. [...] A single drone pilot can effectively search a 20ha area because the time constraint only applies to the drone search. They can take their time evaluating subjects because they are not reliant on the thermal camera of the drone as they already have the potential locations of subjects.

## The basics (Basics.html)

> A search operation is a balance between time constraints and completeness of a search operation.

> It is important to remember that a thermal image is made up of the surface heat of the elements in the image. We need a differential in temperature between a subject and the surroundings to locate it.

> A thermal camera detects the surface heat of an object. Heat does not "shine through" objects in front of it. [...] Similarly, fur may trap body heat, so less is emitted, showing a lower temperature than expected.

> A drone with a thermal camera is more effective with long grass. This is simply because the grass obstructs the subject is less when viewed from above verses from the side.

> Just like physical obstruction, wind can also cause an obstruction in certain circumstances. A layer of moving air may create a cooler barrier between the subject and the camera. This may render the subject invisible to the thermal camera while it may be clearly visible to a normal camera. This is something to keep in mind when considering flying conditions.

> A deer can be spotted from higher than a lapwing nest, so you would have to adjust your flight height based on what you are looking for.

## Search Speed (Height.html)

> The higher you fly, the more you can fit into your view. This means that you can fly faster the higher you fly. A simple demonstration is that in a commercial airliner, looking out the window, the ground is moving very slowly, even though you are flying at almost the speed of sound. If you were to fly the same speed at a lower altitude, the ground would be rushing past in a blur.

> Once you have your height, you can determine a reasonable flight speed. V=0.1h+2 (divide your height by 10 and add 2). This will give you a comfortable speed in m/s

## Thermal camera settings (Settings.html)

> For search, you are only looking for a thermal differential between a subject and the general environment. A white hot or iron hot palette gives you information to make this relatively binary decision; is this the subject of my search or is it the environment.

## Search tools (Tools.html)

> We use DJI M3T and M30T drones for search. These enterprise drones don't have the ability to fly search patterns natively. An area flight route is only used for mapping so the camera cannot be independently controlled.

> We augment our ground teams with thermal cameras so that they can locate subjects in a similar way to the drones. This is useful for when a ground team is in the vicinity of a subject identified by a drone. They can evaluate the heat source themselves without the drone pilot hovering over the spot to guide them.

> Positions, images and markers from the drones and ground teams are shown to all in real time.
